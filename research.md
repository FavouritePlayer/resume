# Structuring a Technical Resume for Competitive SWE and ML Internships (2025–2026)

## TL;DR
- For a UC Berkeley junior with a 3.9 GPA and real internship experience, keep it to ONE page, keep Education first (you're still a student), and make Experience the anchor for SWE-track applications — but build a SECOND, research-first version for ML-research labs where a "Research" section and your symposium presentation lead. Brand names get you past the first skim; project/research depth is what converts the interview.
- The single biggest lever is not brand vs. projects — it is interview-defensible, specific bullets (real numbers where you have them, concrete scope/state-change where you don't). Fabricated metrics are now a documented rejection trigger under LLM-based screening.
- The "shape" genuinely differs by track: SWE-generalist = Education → Experience → Projects → Skills, keyword-matched to the JD; ML-research = Education → Research → Experience → Projects → Skills (with a Presentations line), CV-style framing. Same underlying facts, different ordering and labeling.

## Key Findings

**1. One page is still correct for you.** University career offices are near-unanimous that an undergraduate resume should be one page; the longer CV format is reserved for a fuller academic history. Johns Hopkins' Office for Undergraduate Research states plainly: "An undergraduate resume should not exceed one page," advising students to keep a multi-page *master* resume privately and cut it down to targeted one-page versions. Several 2026 commercial blogs argue the "one-page rule is dead" and cite surveys of experienced professionals preferring two pages — but even the Ladders eye-tracking study that popularized the length debate notes "the two-page rule remains for more experienced job seekers," explicitly framing two pages as for experienced, not early-career, candidates. For a rising junior with 0–2 years of experience, the one-page norm holds.

**2. Section order for an undergrad with real internships: Education → Experience → Projects → Skills for the SWE version.** The Tech Interview Handbook (authored by Yangshun Tay, an ex-Meta Staff Engineer and creator of Blind 75) explicitly states that if you are "still in school or have less than 3 years of experience, you may put Education first." This matches the de-facto university-recruiting convention; deviating can make you "stand out" in a bad way to ATS screeners and non-technical recruiters. Within the body, real internship Experience sits above Projects because paid, real-world experience is the stronger signal for generalist SWE roles.

**3. For the ML-research version, the shape flips toward CV conventions.** Research-track sources rank depth of research experience, presentations, and PI recommendations above polished SWE credentials. Present a dedicated "Research" or "Research Experience" section high on the page, featuring your transformer fine-tuning role and its symposium presentation. A symposium/poster presentation is a legitimate research signal even without a journal publication — the University of Michigan College of Engineering's research-advising guide states that "the strongest applicants will have made presentations about their research at national conferences," and that "publishing a paper, although not necessary, is a fantastic accomplishment."

**4. Brand names vs. project depth — the honest split.** Brand-name companies (Capital One, Bank of America, Microsoft) function primarily as a first-pass filter: they get a recruiter to stop, drive more inbound interest, and "get you into the conversation." But the hiring decision is made on the depth and impact of what you actually did. For your profile, the brand names you already have are an asset for the recruiter skim; the project/research depth is what wins the technical screen.

**5. How screening actually works — two clocks plus a machine gate.** Before a human looks, 97.8% of Fortune 500 companies (489 of 500) use a detectable ATS in 2025, per Jobscan's 5th-edition ATS Usage Report (down slightly from 98.4% in 2024) — and increasingly an LLM/semantic layer sits on top. Then a human does a fast first-pass "rejection scan": recruiters spend an average of 7.4 seconds on the initial screen, per Ladders Inc.'s 2018 Eye-Tracking Study, and that study found "recruiters focus on job titles more than any other element" during the initial scan. Resumes that survive get a longer 30–60 second second pass looking for quantified results and proof. Banks/fintech (Capital One) and big-tech/AI-native companies (Microsoft) both use this pipeline, but with different emphases (below).

**6. Quantification: expected, but not on every line, and never fabricated.** Quantify impact where you genuinely can — resumes with quantified achievements are reported to be roughly 40% more likely to result in an interview (TalentWorks research). But a growing 2025–2026 counter-consensus — reinforced by LLM screeners that now flag fabricated/AI-generated content — holds that "a clear, specific, true bullet with no number often beats a bullet with a weak or forced one. You do not need a metric on every line" (Hiration). When no true metric exists, quantify scope, scale, frequency, or state-change instead of inventing a percentage.

## Details

### Sub-questions 1 & 6 — Experience vs. Projects, and section order

Two questions are bundled here: which bucket to *emphasize*, and which to *order first*.

**Ordering (undergrad, still in school):** Put Education first. The Tech Interview Handbook's recommended ordering for someone "still in school or with less than 3 years of experience" leads with Education. This is corroborated by Berkeley Career Engagement's guidance that college students in conservative or prescribed-path industries (banking, engineering) should use a chronological format with education prominent, and by practitioner threads noting that university-recruiting resumes conventionally put education at top precisely because "it's the de facto format for university recruiting resumes... you don't want to stand out." There is a genuine minority view on Blind that internships should go above education even for new grads ("Internships first. I want to see what you've done. Idc at all what school you went to"), but this is the exception and is riskier for campus-recruiting pipelines and ATS/non-technical screeners.

**Emphasis (SWE-generalist):** Experience above Projects. Real internships are stronger evidence than self-directed projects for a generalist SWE role. The nuance: your Projects section should not be padding — it should be your 2–3 strongest, ideally with links to GitHub or a live demo (the Tech Interview Handbook recommends "at least 2 projects... Always try to link your project name to GitHub"). If a project is weak or generic (a tutorial to-do app), it dilutes rather than helps; multiple hiring voices warn a weak projects section can lower your evaluation. Rule: Experience first and weighted heavier, Projects curated and trimmed.

**Emphasis (ML-research):** This flips by role. For an applied-ML/MLE internship, the applied-hiring lens still favors internships and shipped projects. For a genuine research internship, research experience and presentations lead.

### Sub-questions 3 & 7 — Different "shape," and organizing three experience buckets

Your three buckets: (A) research internship (transformer fine-tuning, symposium presentation); (B) applied engineering internships (full-stack, computer vision); (C) personal/side projects (RL research, CV fundamentals, agentic AI).

**SWE-generalist resume shape:**
- Order: Education → Experience → Projects → Skills.
- Merge buckets A and B into a single reverse-chronological **Experience** section. Do not separate "Research" from "Experience" here — a generalist SWE recruiter reads one "Experience" section fastest, and the research internship still counts as impressive professional experience. Translate the research role into engineering-and-impact language (tools, systems, outcomes) rather than academic framing.
- Bucket C becomes a curated **Projects** section (2–3 best; the agentic-AI and full-stack-relevant ones likely lead because they show shipping ability).
- Keyword-match to the JD (Java, Python, REST APIs, cloud, SQL, etc.).

**ML-research resume shape:**
- Order: Education → **Research** → Experience → Projects → Skills, with a **Presentations** line (and Publications if/when they exist).
- Bucket A is pulled OUT into a dedicated **Research / Research Experience** section at the top of the body, formatted like experience (project title, lab/advisor, dates, methods, outcomes), with the symposium presentation cited either as its own "Presentations" entry (research/CV-style) or folded as a bullet under the research role (applied-style). This is the standard university-career-office recommendation for presentations without formal publications; Binghamton's Fleishman Center advises that "if you do not have many presentations or are applying to industry roles, it may make more sense to summarize your presentation within a bullet under the relevant experience rather than including a formal citation."
- Bucket B stays in **Experience** — the computer-vision internship is especially valuable to foreground for CV/robotics labs.
- Bucket C: the RL research project and CV-fundamentals project are research-adjacent and framed as research-style **Projects** (with code links); agentic-AI projects show currency with the field. For research applications, projects that reproduce SOTA models or train models on interesting datasets are explicitly valued signal (per a Yale CS PhD student's widely shared 2025 undergrad-research guide, built on Karpathy's "PhD survival guide").

The underlying principle: the *substance* doesn't change between versions; only framing, ordering, and labeling do. Methodology → tools/techniques; publications/presentations → outputs/impact; coursework → applied projects.

### Sub-questions 2 & 5 — Brand names, and screening at a bank vs. an AI-native company

**Brand-name weight.** Seven technical recruiters and career coaches told Business Insider (reporter Ana Altchek) that Nvidia, OpenAI, Anthropic, and the FAANG companies are the most powerful résumé names; Dice CEO Art Zeile said FAANG's "rigorous hiring processes, combined with their massive and complex projects, effectively surface the best of the best tech pros." On Blind, the recurring consensus is blunt: brand "gets you into the conversation" and drives inbound recruiter interest, but "hiring decision will be made based on the work you did." Forbes notes brand names also matter more the earlier you are in your career and for candidates with less of a track record — which applies to you. Net: the brand names in your Experience section do real work at the recruiter-skim and ATS-keyword stage; they do NOT substitute for depth once a technical screener reads closely.

**How a bank/fintech (Capital One) screens.** Capital One's student pipeline runs application (via Workday) → recruiter/resume screen → online assessment (a proctored CodeSignal coding test for SWE — commonly reported as 4 questions in 70 minutes, with ~3.25 completed enough to advance; a separate Data Science Challenge for DS roles) → "Power Day" (bundled technical, case, and behavioral interviews). Capital One screens resumes for "relevant coursework, technical projects, and GPA," expects a 3.0+ GPA (no published hard cutoff), and — distinctively — even software engineers get a **case interview**, so bullets that show business/impact framing and communication carry weight. One intern account describes a resume screen followed by a take-home task, then interviews that included a walkthrough of the candidate's own code and a résumé review. Implication: a single resume line must work at the recruiter read, the hiring-manager pre-screen, AND as interview fodder on Power Day.

**How a big-tech/AI-native company (Microsoft) screens.** Microsoft's new-grad/intern loop is recruiter screen (~30 min, culture-heavy, sometimes with a live coding question) → online assessment (Codility/HackerRank, 2–3 problems in 60–90 min) → virtual onsite (4–5 rounds, mostly coding plus a hiring-manager round). A widely shared 2026 account from a Microsoft new-grad candidate describes recruiters sorting resumes into Yes/Maybe (~25%)/No piles at ~4–7 seconds per resume, "scanning for reasons to say no," and largely ignoring summaries/skills/hobbies in that first pass "unless the first six signals are already strong." Implication: formatting clarity and immediately legible titles/impact matter enormously in the first pass; the coding assessment then does much of the technical filtering, so the resume's job is mainly to survive the skim and pass ATS.

**ML/AI research labs (Microsoft CoreAI, DeepMind, robotics/CV labs).** These weight research experience, presentations, and a strong recommendation letter from a research advisor above brand or polished SWE projects. Top labs' undergrad intake is often lightweight on the resume itself — Prof. Pieter Abbeel's UC Berkeley Robot Learning Lab asks undergraduates to "email Lab Director Prof. Pieter Abbeel your resume, your (unofficial) transcript, and 2 paragraphs on why you want to get involved" — with the real signal coming from research fit and the PI relationship. As one PI advises, a recommendation letter "provides future supervisors with much more information about your capabilities than they can glean from scrutinizing your publication record." A publication is a strong plus but not required; symposium/conference presentations are explicitly valued.

### Sub-question 4 — Quantification and metrics

Mainstream advice (Harvard, UC San Diego, and virtually every resume guide) is: start each bullet with an action verb and quantify impact where possible. Data points: a large share of hiring managers pass over resumes with no measurable results (Resume.io cites ~34%), and resumes with quantified achievements are reported to be ~40% more likely to result in an interview (TalentWorks).

There is an important 2025–2026 refinement, and where sources conflict I flag it:
- **Quantify-everything camp:** every bullet should ideally have a number; if a project has zero quantifiable impact, pick a different project (PrepLounge).
- **Defensible-truth camp (now ascendant):** "A clear, specific, true bullet with no number often beats a bullet with a weak or forced one. You do not need a metric on every line" (Hiration). When you lack a true metric, quantify *scope* ("sole scheduler for a 40-person office"), *scale* ("across all 12 branches"), *frequency*, or *state-change* ("cleared the backlog," "zero audit findings across three cycles"). Do NOT invent numbers — fabricated metrics are a red flag recruiters catch in interviews, and newer LLM screeners increasingly flag fabricated/AI-generated content and contextual misalignment. As one guide warns, if you paste your resume into a chatbot and ask it to "add metrics," it "will happily generate defensible-looking numbers you cannot actually defend."

For your ML bullets specifically, real defensible metrics are usually available and expected: accuracy/F1, latency reduction, dataset size, training-time speedup, benchmark deltas ("improved classification accuracy by X%," "trained on 1M examples, reached 90% accuracy," "reduced inference latency 40%"). For methodological research contributions, it's acceptable to describe technique and outcome ("fine-tuned a transformer for [task]; presented results at [symposium]") without a forced business metric.

### Sub-question 8 — One page, formatting, ATS-parsability

**Length:** One page for you (see Key Finding 1). Keep a longer private "master" resume with everything, and cut down to targeted one-page versions per application — the standard university-research-office workflow.

**ATS-parsable formatting (strong consensus across the Tech Interview Handbook, Jobscan, Indeed, and university guides):**
- Single-column layout. No tables, text boxes, columns, headers/footers, images, or graphics — these are the leading cause of parse failures.
- Standard section headings ("Work Experience," "Education," "Skills," "Projects") — no creative headers.
- Standard fonts (Arial, Calibri, Garamond), ≥10pt.
- Build in Word/Google Docs; submit PDF (unless the application specifies .docx) so text stays highlightable/parseable. Some sources prefer .docx for maximum parse reliability; follow the employer's stated preference.
- Reduce margins to ~0.5" rather than using headers/footers to reclaim space.
- Spell out acronyms with the full term at least once (e.g., "Amazon Web Services (AWS)") because ATS keyword search may look for either form.

**Density/whitespace:** Aim for a comfortable scanning density — sources frame this as either ~40–50% whitespace or "use 80–85% of the page for content," differing framings that both warn against a "wall of text." Use 3–5 bullets for your most recent/relevant role, tapering to 2–3 for older/less relevant ones; keep bullets to 1–2 lines. Margins 0.5–1.0"; line spacing ~1.0–1.15; consistent spacing between entries.

**The AI-screening shift (2025–2026), stated carefully:** Most large employers have layered LLM/semantic screening on top of legacy keyword ATS; these systems now understand context, recognize transferable skills without exact-keyword matches, evaluate career trajectory, and can flag fabricated/AI-generated content (per a 2025 arXiv multi-agent resume-screening framework and multiple vendor reports). Treat the *direction* as reliable but treat specific vendor statistics with caution: the oft-cited "75% of resumes never reach a human" traces to Preptel, a resume-software vendor that pitched the figure around 2012 and went out of business in 2013 without publishing a methodology; recruiter surveys instead indicate 90–95% of applications are viewed by a human. The practical upshot is unchanged and reinforced: write natural, specific, keyword-relevant, truthful bullets; don't keyword-stuff (now counterproductive under semantic matching); don't fabricate.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build two master versions now (this summer, before applications open).**
1. Create a single-column, ATS-clean one-page template in Google Docs/Word: standard headings, Arial/Calibri/Garamond ≥10pt, 0.5–0.75" margins, no tables/columns/graphics.
2. **SWE version:** Education (list GPA 3.9 — it's well above the 3.5 threshold the Tech Interview Handbook recommends for listing) → Work Experience (research internship + applied internships merged, reverse-chronological, research role reframed in engineering/impact terms) → Projects (2–3 strongest, GitHub links) → Skills (JD-matched languages/frameworks).
3. **ML-research version:** Education → Research Experience (transformer fine-tuning role featured, with a Presentations line citing the symposium) → Experience (applied CV/full-stack internships) → Projects (RL, CV-fundamentals, agentic AI, with code links) → Skills (PyTorch, TensorFlow, etc.). Add a 1-line headline/summary at top ("CS junior at UC Berkeley focused on ML/CV; fine-tuned transformer models, presented at [symposium]").

**Stage 2 — Content pass.**
4. Rewrite every bullet to the "what you did → how → impact" structure. Put a real, interview-defensible number on every bullet where you can; where you can't, use scope/scale/state-change instead of a fabricated percentage.
5. Curate ruthlessly: cut any project or bullet that fails the "so what?" test. A weak Projects section hurts; trim to your best.
6. For each target company, mirror 2–3 exact keywords/skills from the specific JD in Skills and Experience (Capital One: Java, cloud/AWS, REST, SQL; Microsoft: C#/relevant stack, data structures; ML labs: PyTorch, transformers, CV/RL).

**Stage 3 — Test and route.**
7. Run the plain-text test (paste into a text file; confirm nothing scrambles) and a free ATS scanner (Jobscan/Resume Worded) against 2–3 real JDs; aim for strong keyword match without stuffing.
8. Use the SWE version for Capital One, Bank of America, and Microsoft SWE/Aspire; use the ML-research version for Microsoft CoreAI and robotics/CV research labs. For applied-ML/MLE roles (not pure research), use a hybrid: research-forward but keep applied internships prominent.
9. Get a Berkeley Career Engagement "speed résumé critique" and/or EECS/peer review, and — for research labs — prioritize securing a strong recommendation letter from your research advisor, which for research roles outweighs the resume itself.

**Benchmarks that would change this advice:**
- If you earn a first-author or workshop **publication**, add a Publications section and move fully to a CV-style research resume (and two pages becomes defensible for research applications specifically).
- If you reach 3+ years of full-time-equivalent experience (post-grad), move Experience above Education and drop GPA.
- If a specific application form (Workday/Greenhouse) asks you to re-enter Experience/Education, treat that structured form as the real screen — fill it out completely; it may be parsed instead of your PDF.

## Caveats
- **Source quality varies.** The most reliable guidance comes from the ex-Meta Tech Interview Handbook, university career/research offices (Berkeley, JHU, Princeton, Michigan, Binghamton, WashU), named recruiters quoted in Business Insider/Forbes, and Capital One's/Microsoft's own process descriptions. Much "2026 ATS/AI" commentary comes from resume-tool vendors with a commercial interest; I've flagged contested statistics (the "75%" myth; the "one-page rule is dead" claim, which does not apply to undergraduates).
- **Practitioner forums (Blind, Reddit) are anecdotal** and skew toward experienced hires; I've used them for directional consensus (brand gets you in the door; experience > projects once you have it), not authoritative fact.
- **Company processes change.** Capital One and Microsoft pipeline details are from 2025–2026 write-ups, generally corroborated across sources, but stages/assessments shift year to year; verify against the live posting.
- **Research-lab norms are heterogeneous.** Different labs/PIs weight things differently; some want a CV, some just a resume + transcript + statement. When a lab specifies a format, follow it exactly.
- **Unverified figure:** the frequently cited claim that ~23% of ATS rejections stem from parsing errors (tables/columns/graphics) is widely repeated but I could not trace it to an authoritative primary source; treat it as directional, not precise.
- **The one genuine format disagreement** worth knowing: a minority of practitioners argue against forcing a single page even for early-career candidates. The dominant university-office norm for undergraduates remains one page, which is what I recommend for you.
